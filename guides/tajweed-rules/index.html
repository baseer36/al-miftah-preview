The Tajweed Rules, Explained

Tajweed is the set of rules that govern how the Quran is pronounced. This guide covers the rules a reciter actually needs, grouped the way they are normally taught.

Tajweed by the numbers

Tajweed sounds vast from the outside. In practice it is a finite set of rules, learned in a sensible order, and most of them come up on every page you read.

17articulation pointsWhere each letter is formed: throat, tongue, lips.
5regionsThe empty space, throat, tongue, lips and nasal passage.
4 + 3noon and meem rulesFour for noon saakin and tanween; three for meem saakin.
5qalqalah lettersThe echoing bounce on ق ط ب ج د.

Makharij: where each letter is made

Every Arabic letter has a specific articulation point: a place in the throat, on the tongue, or at the lips where it is formed. There are seventeen of these points across five broad regions.

This is the foundation, and it is the part that cannot be learned from reading. Several Arabic letters have no English equivalent at all, and several pairs sound identical to an untrained ear while being produced in completely different places.

Sifaat: the qualities of the letters

If makharij is where a letter is made, sifaat is how. These qualities distinguish letters that share an articulation point: whether the sound is whispered or voiced, heavy or light, held or released.

Heavy and light

Seven letters are pronounced “heavy”, with the back of the tongue raised. The rest are light.

Whispered and voiced

Some letters let the breath flow; others block it with the voice.

Held and released

Some letters stop the sound at once, others let it continue.

Noon saakin and tanween: four rules

When a noon with no vowel, or a tanween, meets the next letter, one of four things happens. Which one depends only on the letter that follows.

Meem saakin: three rules

Madd: the lengths

Natural madd is held for two counts. The derived madds, when a hamzah or a sukoon follows, extend to four, five or six, depending on the type.

✓
Consistency beats length

A reciter who holds the same madd for different counts on different pages is making a more audible error than one whose count is slightly short but always the same.

Qalqalah and ghunnah

Qalqalah is the echoing bounce on five specific letters when they carry a sukoon. Ghunnah is the nasal sound that accompanies noon and meem in certain rules, held for roughly two counts.

طta (heavy)
بba
جjim
دdal

Both are small and both are immediately noticeable when absent.

The order to learn them in

  1. 1
    Makharij, always first

    The rules below are meaningless if the letters themselves are wrong.

  2. 2
    Noon saakin and tanween

    They occur constantly, so every page becomes practice.

  3. 3
    Meem saakin

    Three rules, quickly learned.

  4. 4
    Madd

    Start with natural madd and make the count consistent.

  5. 5
    Qalqalah, ghunnah and the finishing qualities

    The last layer of polish.

Attempting the rules before the articulation points is the most common way students waste a year.

Questions

Common questions

Can I learn Tajweed on my own?

You can learn the rules on your own. You cannot correct your own pronunciation, because you cannot hear your own errors. That part requires a listener.

How long does it take to learn Tajweed?

Six to twelve months for the full set of rules. The makharij take longest because they are physical habits rather than information.

Is Tajweed obligatory?

Scholars distinguish between the level of Tajweed required to avoid changing the meaning of a word, which is obligatory, and the refinements of beautiful recitation, which are recommended.

Which rules should I learn first?

Makharij first, then noon saakin and tanween, then meem saakin, then madd, then the finishing qualities.

Do I need to read fluently before starting?

You need to read reasonably well. Tajweed applied to halting reading makes both harder.

What is the difference between Tajweed and Hifz?

Tajweed is how the Quran is pronounced. Hifz is memorising it. A student memorising should learn with Tajweed from the first attempt.

Are the counts the same in every school of recitation?

Not exactly. The counts here are those commonly taught for the narration of Hafs; other narrations differ in some madd lengths.
