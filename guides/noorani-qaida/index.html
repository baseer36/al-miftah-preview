Noorani Qaida: The Complete Guide

The Noorani Qaida is the most widely used primer for reading the Quran. This guide walks through what each stage teaches, in order, and where students most often go wrong — so that you know what good progress looks like.

The six stages at a glance

Every edition of the Noorani Qaida moves through the same six stages. What differs between students is not the order but the time spent on each, and where the stumbling points fall.

6stagesLetters, joined forms, vowels, tanween and sukoon, madd, then words.
29lettersEach one learned alone first, then in all its joined shapes.
3–6monthsThe typical range for a full completion.
1rule that matters mostAccuracy first. Speed arrives later, by itself.

Stage 1: the letters alone

The twenty-nine Arabic letters, by name and sound. Nothing is joined yet and there are no vowel marks. The whole job is recognition and accurate sound.

Where it goes wrong: rushing

A student who can name the letters quickly but produces three or four of them from the wrong place in the mouth has not finished this stage, however fast they recite the list.

Stage 2: the joined forms

Arabic letters change shape depending on position. Here the student learns to recognise each letter at the beginning, in the middle and at the end of a word.

!
Where it goes wrong: guessing

Guessing from context rather than reading the shape looks like progress and collapses the moment an unfamiliar word appears.

Stage 3: the short vowels

Fathah, kasrah and dammah. The student must now produce letter and vowel together as a single sound.

!
Where it goes wrong: two beats

A two-beat pronunciation, the letter then the vowel, is the single most common fault at this stage and it has to be corrected by ear.

Stage 4: tanween, sukoon and shaddah

Tanween adds an “n” sound at the end of a word. Sukoon means no vowel at all. Shaddah doubles the letter itself.

Shaddah is pronounced as a genuine doubling, not as a pause, and sukoon must not be given a faint vowel that does not belong there. Both are easy to get slightly wrong and hard to hear in yourself.

Stage 5: maddah and the long vowels

Sounds that must be held, for a specific count. This is the first point at which the student is doing real Tajweed, whether or not it is called that.

✓
Consistency is the rule

Holding a madd for two counts in one line and four in the next is not a small error: it changes the recitation. A steady, slightly short count is better than a long, drifting one.

Stage 6: joining into words and reading aloud

Short words first, then phrases, then the opening verses of the Quran. By the end of this stage a student should be able to read an unfamiliar word correctly on first sight, slowly.

!
Where it goes wrong: moving on too early

Moving on before the reading is accurate, rather than before it is fast. Speed comes later and comes on its own. Accuracy does not.

How to tell real progress from apparent progress

Ask the student to read something they have never seen. If they can decode it slowly and correctly, they are progressing. If they can recite familiar pages fluently but stall completely on new text, they have been memorising rather than reading, and going back a stage now will save a year later.

Real progress looks like

  • Slow, correct reading of a page never seen before
  • The same sound for the same letter, every time
  • Self-correcting when a word does not sound right

Apparent progress looks like

  • Fluent recitation of familiar pages only
  • Stalling on any new text
  • Naming letters fast but from the wrong place

Questions

Common questions

How many lessons are in the Noorani Qaida?

The number of pages varies between editions, but the sequence is the same in all of them: letters, joined forms, short vowels, tanween and sukoon, madd, then words.

How long does the Noorani Qaida take to complete?

Three to six months for most students. The main variable is classes per week.

What age can a child start?

Around four or five for most children, once they can sit for a short lesson and repeat sounds back accurately.

My child can recite the whole Qaida fluently. Are they done?

Not necessarily. Ask them to read a line they have never seen. If they stall, they have memorised the pages rather than learned to read.

Should we repeat a stage if the reading is shaky?

Yes. Going back one stage now is much faster than fixing the same problem a year later, in the Quran itself.

How much should my child practise?

Short and daily. Fifteen minutes of reading aloud every day beats an hour once a week.

What comes after the Qaida?

Nazra: reading the Quran itself from the mushaf, with the same correction in every lesson.
